Somewhere out there, a quotation with your name on it may not exist.

The answer sounded certain. You trusted it, used it, moved on. Nobody has checked it — yet.

You didn't do it out of carelessness. It is how any general-purpose assistant works: trained to sound certain, not to warn you when it is making something up. The answer arrives finished, in the voice of someone who knows — and that voice is the whole problem, because it is identical when the model is right and when it is wrong.

It has already happened, on the record, in a courtroom. In June 2023 a federal court in New York sanctioned a law firm for filing a real brief citing six judicial decisions that had never existed — produced by an AI assistant answering with its usual confidence. The case is public: Mata v. Avianca, Inc., U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York, opinion of 22 June 2023. Nobody involved set out to deceive. The system simply has no way to say "I don't know" — so it didn't.

The question isn't whether this could happen to you. It's that today you have no way of finding out when it already has.

Notice the coincidence: the same hurry that makes you reach for AI every day — because it works, because it is fast, because nobody has time to reread everything — is exactly the hurry that leaves you exposed when the answer is wrong and says nothing.

The problem was never trusting AI. It was trusting an AI that cannot say "I don't know".

Every general assistant answers. Few will tell you where the answer came from.

Before a work goes in, it passes four checks

  1. Public domain, author by author — by date of death, not by assumption. The rule applied is the one where this service is operated: Brazilian copyright law (Law 9,610/98, art. 41), which runs for 70 years after the author's death, the same term used across the European Union. Terms are counted differently elsewhere — in the United States, for instance, by date of publication.
  2. Translation rights, separate from the original — a translator holds rights of their own, running 70 years past the translator's death (art. 14). Without that date confirmed, the volume does not go on sale: today 211 volumes are held back for exactly this reason, even though they exist in the corpus.
  3. Co-authorship, read off the work and not the catalogue — catalogues sometimes list a single author where the title page names collaborators with rights of their own. That is how the Binet-Simon scale (Théodore Simon, died 1961) and a collective work of Dewey's ended up excluded from sale.
  4. Text cleaned of the digitisation notice — libraries such as Google Books and Gallica stamp the file with a rights notice of their own; that notice is stripped before indexing, so it can never come back as a Bibliotheca quotation by accident.

What is this actually for?

Three concrete things: citing an author exactly in a paper, article or lecture; comparing what two authors wrote on the same subject without relying on someone else's summary; and, if you build software, working on top of an indexed corpus instead of cutting up PDFs by hand.

What does the Bibliotheca not do?
