It isn't carelessness on the part of whoever uses it. A general-purpose assistant answers in the voice of someone who knows, and nothing in the answer warns you when it has filled in what it didn't know. That voice can be the same when the model is right and when it is wrong.

Picture a reference list with everything in place: author, work, year, page. Every line has the look of something checked. Look one of them up in the book and the sentence isn't there — not on that page, not on any other. The format was right. What was missing was the text it was supposed to come from.

The question isn't whether this can happen. It's that, from the reader's side of the answer, without opening the book, there is no way to tell when it already has.

Notice the coincidence: the same hurry that makes AI useful — because it works, because it is fast, because nobody has time to reread everything — is the hurry that lets a wrong answer through unannounced.

The problem was never trusting AI. It was trusting an AI that cannot say "I don't know".

This isn't an occasional slip. The answer can sound plausible without quoting correctly, and nothing in it tells the two apart.

Before a work goes in, it passes four checks

  1. Public domain, author by author — by date of death, not by assumption. The rule applied is the one where this service is operated: Brazilian copyright law (Law 9,610/98, art. 41), which runs for 70 years after the author's death, the same term used across the European Union. Terms are counted differently elsewhere — in the United States, for instance, by date of publication.
  2. Translation rights, separate from the original — a translator holds rights of their own, running 70 years past the translator's death (art. 14). Without that date confirmed, the volume does not go on sale: today 182 volumes are held back for exactly this reason, even though they exist in the corpus.
  3. Co-authorship, read off the work and not the catalogue — catalogues sometimes list a single author where the title page names collaborators with rights of their own. That is how the Binet-Simon scale, signed with a co-author still under copyright, and a collective work of Dewey's ended up excluded from sale.
  4. Text cleaned of the digitisation notice — some digital libraries stamp the file with a rights notice of their own; that notice is stripped before indexing, so it can never come back as a Bibliotheca quotation by accident.

Ask a second author what they wrote

The Bibliotheca calls up one author at a time — that is how each one's register and vocabulary, rebuilt from what they actually wrote, stay out of each other's answers. But you can stay in the same conversation: after reading the first, you call the second and ask what their text says about it — with the volume and the exact sentence, when the text has it.

Questions

An ordinary AI assistant already answers this for free. Why pay?

It does answer. The question is whether you check. When the text covers the question, Compendia Bibliotheca cites the author, the volume and the exact sentence; when it doesn't, it says so. You can open the book and check, and the page comes with it when the source volume preserves it. When a generic answer gets a citation wrong, there is no way to know without doing that step yourself. That step is what the Bibliotheca hands you finished.

What is this actually for?

Three concrete things: citing an author exactly in a paper, article or lecture; comparing what two authors wrote on the same subject without relying on someone else's summary; and, if you build software, working on top of an indexed corpus instead of cutting up PDFs by hand.

How do I install it?

In Claude, the Bibliotheca installs as a plugin — in the app, on claude.ai or in Claude Code, by the same route in all three. In ChatGPT there is nothing to download: it opens as a GPT with your licence. You can read the full guide before buying, with the step-by-step and two video lessons.

What does the Bibliotheca not do?

It doesn't interpret an author for you, it doesn't summarise their “essence”, and it doesn't invent a citation when the corpus doesn't cover the question — in that case it tells you it found nothing.
